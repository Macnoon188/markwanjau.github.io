Engineering Log #023

The Provider Lifecycle

Engineering for change.

Date
5 July 2026
Objective
Design a standardized Provider Lifecycle capable of governing how Knowledge Providers are introduced, validated, deployed, maintained, upgraded, deprecated, and ultimately retired without requiring changes to the Cognitive Core.

The Engineering Problem

The earliest Knowledge Providers were developed by the same engineers responsible for the Cognitive Core. Every architectural assumption remained fresh in memory. That situation would not last.

The Provider SDK had already lowered the barrier to extension. Future providers might be developed months — or even years — after the Cognitive Core had stabilized. They might be authored by entirely different engineers, some from external teams, maintained independently of the core platform.

The architecture therefore faced an important question.

How should a provider become part of Jaspa? Equally important, how should it eventually leave?

Without a defined lifecycle, providers risked becoming inconsistent. Installation procedures would differ. Validation standards would drift. Upgrade paths would become unpredictable. Deprecation would rely upon tribal knowledge rather than architectural process.

Extensibility alone was insufficient. An ecosystem requires governance.

Engineering Commentary

Throughout the development of Jaspa, one principle had remained remarkably consistent — every capability should be earned through architectural compliance rather than privileged access. The same philosophy naturally extended to providers.

A provider should not become available simply because its source code exists. It should demonstrate compliance with the architectural contracts established throughout previous milestones: the Provider Manifest describes identity, the Capability Registry enables discovery, the Canonical Data Model ensures interoperability, Acceptance Tests validate behaviour, and Trust metadata preserves evidence quality.

Likewise, removing a provider deserved equal engineering discipline. Retiring a provider should never require modifications to planners, schedulers, or orchestration logic. Instead, retirement should simply represent another state within the provider lifecycle.

The platform grows by changing its ecosystem — not by rewriting its core.

Engineering Principles

Engineering Principle 023
Every provider follows the same journey.

Consistency across the provider lifecycle reduces operational complexity and ensures that architectural quality does not depend upon who authored a particular provider.

Engineering Principle 024
Joining the ecosystem should require compliance, not permission.

Providers become part of Jaspa by satisfying architectural contracts rather than receiving privileged treatment from the Cognitive Core. This preserves fairness, predictability, and long-term maintainability.

Architecture Decision Record

ADR-018Accepted

Title

Standardize the Provider Lifecycle

Context

The Provider SDK and extensibility framework significantly increased the number of potential providers entering the ecosystem. Without a consistent lifecycle, onboarding, maintenance, and retirement risked becoming inconsistent across implementations.

Decision

Every provider shall progress through a defined architectural lifecycle governed by standardized validation, certification, deployment, maintenance, and retirement procedures. Lifecycle state transitions shall occur independently of the Cognitive Core.

Consequences

The provider ecosystem gains predictable operational behaviour while preserving architectural stability. Future providers inherit a repeatable engineering process regardless of implementation language or knowledge domain.

Engineering Artifacts

Provider Lifecycle

Provider Developed ↓ Manifest Validation ↓ Capability Certification ↓ Registry Registration ↓ Acceptance Testing ↓ Production Ready ↓ ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐ ↓ ↓ Version Upgrade Routine Maintenance └─────────────┬─────────────┘ ↓ Deprecation Notice ↓ Provider Retirement

Every provider follows the same architectural progression regardless of its internal implementation. The lifecycle itself becomes another stable contract within the platform.

Lifecycle Responsibilities

Provider Author ↓ Implements Provider ↓ Publishes Manifest ↓ Certification Pipeline ↓ Capability Registry ↓ Planner Discovery ↓ Operational Monitoring ↓ Lifecycle Management

Lifecycle governance exists alongside cognitive execution rather than within it. The Planner remains responsible for selecting capabilities. Lifecycle management remains responsible for determining whether those capabilities are eligible for selection.

Lessons Learned

  • Ecosystems mature when participation becomes predictable.
  • Architectural governance scales more effectively than implementation-specific procedures.
  • Retirement deserves the same engineering attention as deployment.
  • Standardized lifecycle management reduces institutional knowledge — the architecture itself should provide the answers.

// The Next Question

A standardized lifecycle ensures that providers can be introduced and retired consistently. It does not, however, guarantee that providers developed months or years apart will continue to work together.

How can Jaspa support new provider capabilities without breaking those already deployed?

The next engineering challenge would focus on version compatibility — designing an architecture that allows the platform to evolve while preserving the stability upon which its ecosystem depends.
